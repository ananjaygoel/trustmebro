## The Details

Animals begin to behave as they would at dusk: Crickets chirp and bees go back to their hives. People gather outside, experiencing the event together as they all look up .

Samantha Thompson , astronomy curator at the Smithsonian’s National Air and Space Museum , saw a total eclipse for the first time in 2017. She was in South Carolina with her family.

## Why This Matters

She and her mother both cried. “It was just the most clean thing that I had ever seen,” Thompson says. The fleeting nature of these spectacular events adds to their allure.

Scientists and researchers are watching this development closely.

## Key Takeaways

- Totality can last from ten seconds to around seven minutes , and it happens somewhere on Earth only once about every 18 months , on average.
- But capturing a total eclipse with a camera can freeze that moment forever in astronomical history.
- The first successful image of a total solar eclipse was taken in the 19th century—a daguerreotype by Johann Julius Friedrich Berkowski .
- This early photography technique produced a single, one-of-a-kind image on a silver-coated piece of copper treated with light-sensitive chemicals.

## The Bottom Line
