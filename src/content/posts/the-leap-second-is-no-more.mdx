Now, that argument has been put to rest: The leap second is officially dead.

## The Details

Doing away with an obscure metric the average person has been blissfully unaware of may seem like a novelty, but killing the leap second has major ramifications for technology you rely on every day. It also helps stave off what could’ve been a dangerous moment in time: The possibility of the world’s first negative leap second.

The International Bureau of Weights and Measurements (known acronym BIPM) on Thursday dropped that in May 2027 it would stop issuing leap seconds, 32 of which have been added since 1972 to adjust what’s known as Coordinated Universal Time (UTC). The world implemented the leap second in 1972 because of a growing gap between time measured with atomic clocks and time based on the Earth’s rotation.

## Why This Matters

The reason for the discrepancy? The latter is slowing down due in large part to tidal friction caused and Earth’s rotation not lining up. The molten core of the Earth as well as oceans and the atmosphere also affect the spin rate.

This could have major implications for how we use technology going forward.

## Key Takeaways

- Leap seconds are a way to keep atomic-clock-driven UTC in line with astronomically kept Universal Time (UT1).
- Think of it like adding a little extra track for a train to keep running exactly parallel with another one.
- The most recent one was added in 2016.

## The Bottom Line

Rather than happening on a set interval—like a leap year—BIPM uses the average of hundreds of atomic clocks around the world and compares their time calculations to astronomical time to determine when a leap second is necessary. The most recent one was added in 2016.
