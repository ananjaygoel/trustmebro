## The Details

In some creatures, the vital fluid is purple; in others it’s yellow, green or even white. What imparts these different colors to the liquid of life?

To find out, Knowable magazine waded into the research and spoke with a couple of scientists who have studied the stuff. Here’s what’s known about the science behind the hues.

## Why This Matters

Bizarre blues and purples The rich red of vertebrate blood comes from hemoglobin, a protein in red blood cells that contains iron atoms. Hemoglobin’s job is to deliver oxygen—and its iron atoms, which grab onto oxygen, make our blood look red. When iron atoms are attached to oxygen, blood is a bright cherry hue; when less oxygen is bound, it’s a muddier brown-red.

The scientific community tends to find developments like this significant.

## Key Takeaways

- But many creatures don’t use hemoglobin.
- Octopuses , squid and crustaceans such as shrimp and crabs, for instance, rely on a protein called hemocyanin to ferry oxygen around.
- Hemocyanin has copper atoms—which look blue when bound to oxygen—instead of hemoglobin’s iron.
- Hemocyanin doesn’t bind to oxygen as efficiently as hemoglobin does.

## The Bottom Line

But it functions especially well in cold temperatures, such as those found in the deep ocean. And it can have more jobs than carrying oxygen in the creatures that use it: Research suggests that hemocyanins can play an immunity role , aiding in healing wounds, for example.
