## The Details

2-mile stretch of white sand on Aldabra atoll in the western Indian Ocean. We’re each here for a month on different projects: Talma is volunteering with the atoll’s marine research program, and I’m here to study its 180,000 giant tortoises for a book.

As Talma leads us out along a path that parallels the beach, we detour around one of these sleeping giants, with its head flopped down on the short, cropped grass. Aldabra, 400 miles off the eastern coast of Africa, is one of the few places on Earth where giant tortoises might cross paths with their distant seafaring cousins, green sea turtles, which are the target of our search tonight.

## Why This Matters

Green turtles nest on Aldabra year-round, but fewer are found this time of year. Our red-beam flashlights—the only artificial light out here—sweep the smooth sand, pale in moonlight. The females, nesting mostly on the atoll’s outer rim, leave distinctive tracks: alternating left-right gouges in the sand up the beach, made at a time, that look like tire tread marks from a monster truck.
