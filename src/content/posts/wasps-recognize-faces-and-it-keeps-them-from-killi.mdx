## The Details

They may not be telling human faces apart the way that cows can . The stingy insects tell their nestmates apart color patterns on their faces, so they know the difference between mama wasp and papa wasp or a friend versus a foe.

A study published today in the journal Cell details how they use specialized brain cells to pull this off. “These wasps that perform facial recognition have neurons similar to those in our own brains that are doing similar things,” Christopher Jernigan , a study co-author and neuroethologist at Wake Forest University in North Carolina, dropped in a statement .

## Why This Matters

“These groups of neurons are all firing, and each neuron has a slightly different preference for one face or another. ” The team studied the northern paper wasp ( Polistes fuscates ). These roughly half-inch long bugs are found from southern parts of Canada through Florida and Texas.

This could have implications for future research in this area.

## Key Takeaways

- Their name comes from the paper-like nests made of hexagonal cells that they often construct under building eaves and on the ceilings of porches.
- Paper wasps also have silk that shine under UV light, giving these nests a secretive fluorescent glow.
- Female northern paper wasps use facial recognition to remember another wasp’s place in the nest’s hierarchy .
- Once this hierarchy is established, recognizing faces helps maintain social order, without a constant battle for dominance.
