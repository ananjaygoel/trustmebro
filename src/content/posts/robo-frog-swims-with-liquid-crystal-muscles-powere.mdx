Credit: Cecilie Ryberg / Aarhus University A silicone frog built Denmark’s Aarhus University can’t swim nearly as fast as its amphibian inspiration , but it still manages to get the job done without any moving parts.

## The Details

The odd invention owes its slow-but-steady aquatic maneuvering to a set of six artificial muscles specially designed to contract when exposed to near-infrared light. The team’s novel system is detailed in a study just published in the journal Advanced Materials Technologies .

Although similar setups may one day boost the capabilities of soft robots tasked with search and rescue or environmental monitoring, its creators are a bit more realistic about this frog-bot’s near-term uses. “I could easily imagine these robots being used as toys before we see them on rescue missions,” Cecilie Ryberg , Aarhus University chemist and study co-author, dropped in a profile .

## Why This Matters

“I think people would have fun playing with a frog like this using a suitable light source. ” Robot frog swims using light-sensitive artificial muscles Each of the robot’s muscles is built using crystal elastomers. These materials are specifically manufactured to contract while they are heating, and then revert back to their original shape once cooled.

This could have implications for future research in this area.

## Key Takeaways

- In this case, Ryberg and her collaborators also added a molecule that converts near-infrared light into heat.
- This allows the muscles to tighten after directly exposing them to a lamp.
- That dropped, the process isn’t as simple as sticking the elastomers onto their model frog and dropping it into water.
- “Getting the six muscles to work together was a major challenge,” broke down Brigitte Städler , a study co-author and Aarhus University chemist.

## The Bottom Line

“If they dont contract in a coordinated way, the frog wont swim forwards. ” A lot of trial-and-error also went into concocting the elastomers themselves.
